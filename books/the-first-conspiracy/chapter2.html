The First Conspiracy Why We Traded Freedom for Walls

Chapter Two

The Golden Age of Wandering

For two hundred thousand years, humans lived without bosses, without borders, and without the idea that anyone could own land. Then something changed — and that change was not inevitable.

There is a version of human society that works.

It does not require a state, a police force, a legal system, or any formal mechanism for the resolution of disputes. It does not produce kings or chiefs or any permanent hierarchy of status. It does not generate wealth inequality, or social classes, or the concept of poverty. It produces — in fact, it requires — something that the societies built on top of it have found very difficult to achieve: genuine equality.

This is not a theoretical description. It is a description of how a substantial fraction of the human species actually lived until very recently — within the last twelve thousand years, a period that represents roughly five percent of anatomically modern human existence. The societies in question — mobile hunter-gatherer bands — were not primitive versions of what came later. They were fully functional, stable, and in many respects remarkably successful social systems that had been refined over a time span so long that it exceeds the entire written record of human history by a factor of approximately forty.

The anthropologist James Woodburn spent more than forty years studying one of the few remaining groups of such hunter-gatherers — the Hadza of Tanzania — and the picture that emerges from his research is one of a society that solved the fundamental problems of human organization without creating the structures of power that characterize settled civilization. His conclusion, published in a landmark 1982 paper titled "Egalitarian Societies," is that these societies are not merely lacking inequality. They actively prevent it. Not through social pressure or cultural norm, but through a set of social mechanisms so deeply embedded that the very concept of status accumulation would be incomprehensible to their members.

The immediate-return system — in which food is shared within hours of being obtained, in which there is no storage of surplus, and in which every adult has equal access to all the resources of the group's territory — is not a failure to develop property concepts. It is a deliberate choice, maintained across generations and across cultures, not to develop them. And the reason is not difficult to understand once you see it: the immediate-return system produces a society in which no one has power over anyone else, and in which all adults are genuinely free to leave at any time. These are not separate facts. In this context, they are the same fact.

The Mechanics of Freedom

In the Hadza model — and the evidence suggests it is broadly representative of mobile forager societies generally — the fundamental unit of social organization is the band, typically numbering between twenty and fifty individuals. The band's composition is fluid: people move between bands freely, typically changing membership several times in a lifetime, and almost always for reasons that the individuals themselves would describe as personal rather than political. There is no formal process for joining or leaving a band. There is no authority — individual, collective, or ritual — that has the power to prevent someone from leaving or to prohibit a new person from arriving.

The implications of this fluidity are profound. Because no one is trapped in their membership of a particular group, no one can be dominated by it. The person who attempts to exercise power over others in a Hadza band will find that others simply leave. The person who attempts to accumulate resources will find that those resources are shared before they can be stored. The person who attempts to establish a permanent position of authority will find that the position does not exist — that there is no social role that confers the right to give orders that others are obligated to obey.

This is not because the Hadza are somehow more moral or more enlightened than people in settled societies. It is because the social structure they live in makes the exercise of domination structurally impossible. Power requires the ability to enforce obedience, which requires control over resources or social mechanisms that the subject cannot access independently. In the immediate-return system, the key resources — food, water, knowledge of the landscape, the location of seasonal resources — are distributed so widely that no individual or small group can control them. And the mobility of the group means that any attempt to coerce a person can be answered simply by moving away.

The result is a society with no rulers, no bosses, no permanent inequalities of status or wealth. Not because humans in this context are saintly or primitive, but because the conditions that make domination possible have not been established. The game is set up in a way that makes the behavior of domination not rewarding — which is to say that the immediate-return system is not a failure to develop hierarchies. It is an alternative strategy for organizing human cooperation that is as sophisticated, as stable, and as well-adapted to its environment as any civilization that has come after it.

The Decision to Stay

The transition from this model to the settled, hierarchical, agriculture-based societies that characterize the Neolithic revolution is not a single event. It is a process — one that appears to have begun in multiple locations independently, and one that took several thousand years to complete. And it is a transition that required a specific set of conditions to become possible.

The first condition was a reason to stay. Not a desire for comfort or convenience, but a genuine reason — grounded in the reward chemistry of the human nervous system — why a person would choose to remain in one place rather than continuing to move. The ritual enclosure at Karahan Tepe and Göbekli Tepe provided this reason. A person who entered those spaces and experienced the awe response — the neurological event that makes an individual feel small and connected at the same time — did not leave the same person who entered. They left with a specific, powerful, and chemically reinforced desire to return. And because the space was fixed — because the pillars did not move — the desire to return became a form of commitment to the location.

The second condition was a reason to stop moving completely. The desire to return is not the same as the decision to stay. For a mobile forager band, the decision to stay — to abandon mobility entirely, to commit to a single location as a permanent residence — would have represented an enormous change in lifestyle. It would have required giving up the flexibility and freedom that the immediate-return system provided, and adopting the risks and constraints of a different way of living. For this decision to become collectively compelling, the benefit of staying would have had to clearly outweigh the cost.

The benefit, in the context of these early enclosures, was the collective reward of the ritual experience. A person who stayed near the enclosure could attend the gatherings that produced the awe response regularly — not once a season, but regularly, over and over. The neurochemical reinforcement of repeated experience would have strengthened the association between the place and the reward, making the desire to remain near it more powerful with each iteration. At some point — and the archaeological record does not show exactly when — the desire to remain near the enclosure became stronger than the desire to continue moving, and the mobile band became a settlement.

What was lost in this transition is the central question of this book. The immediate-return system provided genuine equality, genuine freedom, and genuine independence from domination. The settled system that replaced it provided something else — a different kind of social bond, a different relationship to place, a different set of psychological rewards. The trade was made. And the people who made it — and every generation since — lived with the consequences.

The "Stay" Decision

Once a settlement exists, a new kind of social dynamic becomes possible: the accumulation of group-specific knowledge, the development of shared rituals that are particular to the place and the people, the construction of a collective identity that is rooted in the specific landscape and history of the group. These are not available to a mobile band — the knowledge is too distributed, the history is too diffuse, the identity is too fluid to support the kind of shared meaning that a settled group can develop.

This is the other side of the trade. Freedom for belonging. The mobile forager gave up the freedom to leave, to move, to choose differently — and in exchange, received the experience of being part of something that was specific, particular, and irreplaceable. This is what made the trade feel invisible. The reward of belonging is not abstract. It is immediate, powerful, and undeniable. And because it is experienced as a discovery rather than a transaction, the fact that it was purchased — that something was given up in order to obtain it — does not register.

The anthropologist Marshall Sahlins wrote about what he called the "original affluent society" — the hunter-gatherer band that has no conception of insufficiency because it has no standard of comparison. The affluence is not material. It is a condition of the social system: there is enough, because sharing is the default; there is no poverty, because there are no accumulations; there is no hunger, because the group eats together. This is what was traded. Not poverty for wealth. Not scarcity for abundance. Affluence for something else — something that was compelling enough that whole populations chose it, and compelling enough that they have never chosen to go back.

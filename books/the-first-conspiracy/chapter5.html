The First Conspiracy

Chapter Five

The First Borders

The first border was not built. It was imagined. And the moment "inside" existed, so did exclusion. The line drawn in the dirt was the most consequential mark humans ever made.

The first border was not built. It was imagined.

Before there was a wall, there was a concept. A line in the mind that separated what belonged from what did not. The physical marking of that line — with stones, with stakes, with a furrow drawn in earth — was an expression of something that had already taken shape in the social imagination. Territory came after territory was already believed in.

The human capacity for territorial thinking is not unique — it is shared with many other social animals, from wolves to chimpanzees to lions. What is unique about humans is the complexity and abstraction of the territorial concept: the ability to draw lines not just around immediate resources but around abstract spaces, to define membership not by physical proximity but by shared identity, and to enforce these abstractions through social mechanisms rather than purely physical aggression.

Understanding how that abstraction developed — from animal territory to human borders — is the key to understanding how the first walls were built in the mind rather than in stone.

Territoriality as Biology

This is ours. That is not. And suddenly, space changes meaning.

Territorial behavior in animals is one of the most well-documented phenomena in ethology. Wolves maintain hunting ranges that they defend against other packs. Some bird species sing from fixed positions to signal ownership of feeding territory. Chimpanzees patrol the boundaries of their home ranges and attack members of neighboring groups who cross into them.

The biological function of territory is resource management: a territory big enough to feed a group, when defended against competitors, provides a reliable food supply without the costs of constant migration. The cost is defense — the energy and risk of engaging rivals at the boundary — which is balanced against the benefit of reliable access to the resources inside.

Human territorial behavior shows the same structure, but with three features that distinguish it from non-human territoriality. First, humans can communicate the concept of territory abstractly — a person who has never seen a specific area can know it is ours and feel a strong emotional response to that knowledge. Second, human territory can include people who have never been there, who identify with it despite never having physically occupied it. Third, human territory can be expanded or contracted through negotiation, conquest, or agreement — it is a social construct as much as an ecological one.

These features are products of the specific cognitive capacities of the human species: large-scale social cooperation enabled by shared symbolic systems, theory of mind sufficient to understand that others have beliefs about spaces they have not visited, and language sufficient to transmit complex geographic and political information across time and space.

The pre-agricultural human territory was not a fixed area with permanent boundaries. It was a network of resource areas connected by movement routes — a seasonal pattern of utilization rather than a static map. Different groups had overlapping ranges, and the boundaries between them were zones of interaction rather than lines of division. This flexibility was an adaptation to the variability of natural resources.

"The moment territory becomes fixed, it becomes property. The moment property becomes inheritable, it becomes power. The moment power becomes centralized, it becomes the state. And every one of these steps follows from the decision to stay."

— Dr. Peter G. T. F. Weng, Political Anthropology, London School of Economics

The Invention of Exclusion

The moment inside exists, so does exclusion.

The semantic structure of territorial thinking contains an inherent paradox. The concept of inside requires the concept of outside — they are defined in relation to each other, and neither can exist without the other. When a human group first drew a permanent boundary and declared the space within it to be ours, they simultaneously created the category of not ours — a category that initially applied to neighboring groups who had previously moved freely through the area, and that expanded from there to include anyone who was not a member of the defining community.

The creation of the inside/outside distinction is the moment at which the social contract became territorial. Prior to this moment, the social group was defined by affiliation — by relationships, shared knowledge, mutual aid, and ongoing social interaction. Membership was a function of participation. After the territorial distinction was drawn, membership became a function of location and identity: you belonged if you were inside the boundary and shared the identity of those who drew it.

This shift had profound consequences for the structure of human cooperation. Before territorial fixedness, a group could expand or contract based on the number of people who chose to participate in it. After territorial fixedness, the group had a defined size and a defined identity that persisted beyond the participation of any individual member.

The psychological mechanism that makes territorial exclusion so powerful is in-group/out-group bias — one of the most well-documented and robust findings in social psychology. Humans reliably favor members of their own group over members of other groups, even when the basis for group membership is arbitrary, trivial, or randomly assigned.

The tribal psychology that makes this response so strong evolved in the context of inter-group competition for resources. In an environment where neighboring groups were potential competitors for food, water, shelter, and territory, the ability to rapidly identify and cooperate with in-group members while maintaining suspicion toward out-group members would have conferred significant survival advantages. The problem is that this ancient neural architecture does not distinguish between tribal groups and nation-states — it responds to the social category in the same way regardless of scale.

The first border was a line drawn in the dirt. But it was drawn in the nervous system before it was drawn on the ground. And the moment both lines were complete — the neural border and the physical one — the world changed in a way that has never been reversed.

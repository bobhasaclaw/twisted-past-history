The First Conspiracy

Chapter Ten

Living in the Ruins

We never left. We only refined it. How the ancient pillars became glass towers, the rituals became invisible, and the walls we built in the mind became the only walls we can no longer see.

We believe we are modern. But the structure remains.

Stand at the base of a skyscraper in any major city and look up. The glass tower rises above you — sleek, engineered to tolerances measured in millimeters, connected to global networks of information and capital. The people inside it carry devices that would have seemed like magic to anyone in the twentieth century, let alone the tenth. The system is different. The material is different. The scale is different. But the structure beneath the surface — the architecture of human organization that makes this building possible — is the same structure that was built in stone at Karahan Tepe twelve thousand years ago.

This is not a metaphor. It is a literal description of the inheritance. The skyscraper requires a stable workforce that has been trained from childhood to arrive at a specific location at a specific time, to perform specialized tasks in coordinated sequences, and to accept the authority of a hierarchical management structure. These behaviors are not natural human behaviors — they are cultivated adaptations, developed over years of schooling and socialization, that reshape the nervous system to function within a specific institutional environment. The person who works in the tower has been domesticated to it.

The tower also requires an infrastructure of shared meaning — a narrative framework that explains why the work being done inside the tower matters, why the authority structure is legitimate, and why the people inside it should continue coming back every day. This framework is maintained through corporate culture, professional identity, and the symbolic systems of the institution — its logos, its rituals, its shared language and references. These are the modern equivalent of the carved pillars at Karahan Tepe: physical structures, material symbols, that trigger the neurochemical responses associated with belonging, status, and connection to something larger than the individual self.

The Invisible Architecture

The pillars are now glass. The rituals are invisible.

What has changed in twelve thousand years is not the mechanism of control — it is the sophistication of its presentation. The Neolithic ritual enclosure used physical architecture, fire, and acoustic resonance to produce its effects. The modern institution uses social architecture, digital media, and psychological design to produce equivalent effects. The technology is different. The underlying engineering is the same.

Social media platforms are, in structural terms, ritual enclosures. They gather large populations in a shared space. They induce specific emotional states through the design of the interface — variable reward schedules that mirror the dopaminergic conditioning of the Neolithic gathering. They create in-group/out-group dynamics through the formation of communities, the marking of identity through profiles and posts, and the enforcement of belonging through social metrics. They provide interpretation through the algorithm — feeding each user a personalized narrative about what their experience means.

The key difference is that the modern ritual enclosure operates at a scale that the Neolithic architect could not have imagined, with a personalisation precision that no previous technology has achieved. The ritual at Karahan Tepe produced awe in a group of perhaps hundreds of people who stood in the same physical space. The algorithm produces awe in hundreds of millions of people simultaneously, in different physical locations, through individually tailored experiences that have been optimized to trigger each user's specific psychological vulnerabilities.

The invisibility of modern ritual is not accidental. The effectiveness of the ancient mechanism depended on participants believing that the awe experience was genuine — that something real was happening in the stone enclosure. The modern mechanism achieves the same end through a different means: by making the ritual so seamlessly integrated into daily life that it is no longer perceived as ritual at all. Checking your phone is not experienced as attending a gathering. It is experienced as checking the time. The ritual is hidden in the mundane.

"The most successful control systems are the ones that the controlled do not recognize as control systems. We do not feel ourselves to be in a ritual. We feel ourselves to be making choices."

— Dr. Federico Aldegheri, Behavioural Architecture, UCL

The Question of Recognition

We never left. We only refined it.

The final and perhaps most significant observation that this documentary offers is a question, not an answer: can a domesticated population recognize its own domestication? Can the modern Homo sapiens see through the architecture of control that surrounds them — when that architecture is also the medium through which all their thinking takes place?

The analogy to the fox is instructive. Belyaev's domesticated foxes did not know they were domesticated. They had no access to the experience of being wild, because their neural development had been shaped by the selection process that made them tractable — and that same development made the alternative inconceivable. They behaved in ways that felt natural to them, because the neural architecture that produced those behaviors was the only architecture they had.

Modern humans are in a similar position — not because of deliberate selective breeding, but because of the accumulated effect of twelve thousand years of living inside systems that reshape the nervous system to be compatible with them. The behaviors that feel most natural to us — the need for status, the drive to accumulate, the identification with large-scale political collectives, the sense of anxiety when those collectives are threatened — are not natural human behaviors. They are adaptations to a specific environment, and they feel natural only because they are our only available mode.

What Karahan Tepe offers, in this context, is not a path to escape — because escape, for a domesticated species, is not physically possible. But Karahan Tepe offers something more valuable than escape: it offers perspective. It shows us that the mechanism we experience as natural, universal, and inevitable is actually an invention — one that was made by specific people, in a specific time and place, for specific purposes. And that recognition is the beginning of something that the control architecture cannot produce by design: the ability to see the architecture as architecture, and to choose, with full awareness of what is being chosen, whether to serve it or to refuse it.

The pillars are still there. They are made of glass now, and they are connected by invisible threads of data and dopamine. But they are still pillars. And the question they have always asked — the question that stands at the center of Karahan Tepe, of Göbekli Tepe, of every temple and cathedral and corporate headquarters that has been built in the twelve thousand years since — remains unanswered.

The question is not why we built them. The question is whether we could have chosen otherwise. And whether we can choose otherwise now.

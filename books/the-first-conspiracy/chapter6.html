The First Conspiracy

Chapter Six

The Biology of the Trade

Belonging is chemical. We did not choose freedom or its replacement — we were driven. The reward systems that made civilization possible are the same ones that make leaving it feel like dying.

Belonging is chemical. Dopamine. Oxytocin. Reward systems. The decision to stay — to abandon the mobile life and commit to a fixed location — was not made by the human mind as a rational calculation of long-term benefits. It was made by the human body, in response to a set of chemical signals that had been evolved over millions of years to do exactly one thing: make social bonding feel necessary enough to override self-interest.

To understand why the trade was invisible — why it felt like no trade at all — we need to understand what these chemicals do, and how the architecture of Neolithic ritual space was specifically designed to trigger them.

Dopamine: The Anticipation Molecule

Return becomes reward. Participation becomes acceptance. And acceptance becomes dependency.

Dopamine is not, despite its popular reputation, the chemical of pleasure. It is the chemical of anticipation — the neurotransmitter that drives seeking behavior, that makes the approach to a reward feel compelling rather than the reward itself. This distinction is important. Dopamine does not make you feel satisfied when you arrive. It makes you feel driven to continue when you are on your way.

The dopaminergic system evolved to respond to unpredictable rewards — specifically, rewards that are delivered intermittently, with variable timing and variable magnitude. This is the structure of the reward schedule that produces the strongest behavioral conditioning: not a reward delivered every time an action is performed, but a reward delivered at intervals that cannot be perfectly predicted, so that the subject cannot stop performing the action because they can never be certain that the next repetition will not be rewarded.

Early ritual gatherings exploited this mechanism deliberately. The enclosure at Karahan Tepe was not a place where a person could predict exactly what would happen — the experience was variable, the effects were not fully controllable, and the outcomes were not guaranteed. But one thing was reliably true: the person who returned had the experience of being present, and the person who did not return had the experience of being absent. And that experience — of participating versus not participating, of belonging versus being outside — was itself a reward that became more valuable with each iteration.

"Dopamine is not about pleasure. It is about the pursuit of pleasure. And that distinction is everything — because the pursuit is endless, while the pleasure is always brief. Every ritual system ever constructed has been, whether its architects knew it or not, an engineering of the dopaminergic response."

— Dr. Anna R. Bhargava, Computational Neuroscience, MIT

Oxytocin: The Bonding Chemical

We did not lose freedom. We replaced it — with reward.

Oxytocin is the neuropeptide most strongly associated with social bonding — the formation of attachment between individuals, between parent and child, between group members. It is released during positive social interactions: physical touch, eye contact, synchronized activity, shared experience. It reduces anxiety, increases trust, and specifically increases cooperative behavior toward in-group members while simultaneously increasing defensive behavior toward out-group members.

The ritual enclosures at Karahan Tepe and Göbekli Tepe were, among other things, oxytocin-releasing machines. A person who entered the enclosure, stood alongside others who were having similar experiences, heard the same sounds, saw the same firelight, and felt the same mixture of awe and fear — that person's oxytocin system was being strongly activated. The shared experience created bonds between people that were not based on kinship, not based on reciprocity, not based on any rational calculation. They were based on the pure neurochemical fact of having felt the same thing, in the same place, at the same time.

This bond was, in a specific sense, the first form of social capital. It created obligations and expectations that existed without any explicit agreement, without any written contract, without any formal enforcement mechanism. The person who had experienced the enclosure together with others felt an automatic, chemically-driven sense of connection to those others. And because the experience was specifically located — it required going to the enclosure to have it — this bond was also tied to the place. Leaving the group meant losing access to the place, which meant losing access to the chemical experiences that the place reliably produced.

This is why the trade felt invisible. The reward systems that the enclosure triggered were more powerful than anything the mobile life had offered. Freedom — the ability to move, to leave, to choose differently — was real, but it was abstract. The feelings produced by the ritual space were immediate, powerful, and undeniable. The brain chose the known reward over the abstract freedom, and called it preference.

The First Conspiracy Why We Traded Freedom for Walls

Chapter Nine

The Sunk Cost Trap

Why didn't we leave? Because by then, we couldn't. The more we built, the harder it became to walk away — and that was the point.

Why didn't we leave? Because by then, we couldn't.

The sunk cost fallacy is one of the most robust and well-documented cognitive biases in behavioral economics. It describes the tendency to continue investing in a failing course of action because of what has already been invested — rather than because of what the investment is expected to return. The classic example: a person who has spent hundreds of dollars on a non-refundable concert ticket will drive through a snowstorm to attend a show they no longer want to see, because the alternative is to accept the money as permanently lost. The money is already gone. The decision to attend or not attend cannot recover it. But the feeling of loss makes the attendance feel necessary.

The difference between the individual sunk cost fallacy and the civilizational sunk cost trap is one of scale, not structure. An individual who has wasted money on a concert ticket makes bad decisions for a day or two. A population that has invested decades of labor in permanent structures makes bad decisions for generations.

Karahan Tepe is a sunk cost trap. The labor required to quarry, transport, and erect the massive T-shaped pillars — estimated by archaeologist Klaus Schmidt's team at hundreds of thousands of person-hours across a population of perhaps three to five hundred people — represents a collective investment that could not be recovered by moving. If the group that built Karahan Tepe decided to leave, they would be leaving behind the entire output of their collective labor. They would be starting over — not with nothing, but with only what they could carry.

This is the key design principle of the first permanent structures: they were built to be immovable. Not because the builders anticipated future theft. They were built to be immovable because immovability was the mechanism by which commitment was enforced. A tent can be packed. A stone pillar cannot. The structure that cannot be moved makes the decision to stay permanent in a way that no verbal agreement or social contract ever could.

The Logic of Irreversibility

The more we built, the harder it became to walk away.

The commitment mechanism at work in early settlement is a specific form of credible commitment — a term used in game theory and political science to describe a promise or obligation that is enforced by physical constraints rather than by the threat of punishment. Unlike a contract, which is enforced by a third party, a credible commitment is enforced by the structure of the situation itself. Once you have made it, you cannot undo it without suffering a loss so large that backing out is never the rational choice.

The stone pillar at Karahan Tepe is a credible commitment device. By quarrying and erecting it, the community made a commitment to remain in the area that was physically enforced by the pillar's immovability. No individual, no family, no faction could reverse this commitment unilaterally — because the structure remained, and the group that remained in the area maintained the physical proof of their collective investment.

This mechanism has a direct parallel in the modern world. When a government issues sovereign debt, it creates a credible commitment backed by the entire future output of the national economy. The bond holder does not need to trust that the government will choose to repay. They can trust that the government cannot afford not to: the political cost of default is so high that the rational choice is always to service the debt. The commitment is enforced by the structure of the international financial system.

What the pillar and the bond have in common is that they transform a voluntary decision into an involuntary obligation. The group chose to build the pillar, once. After that, the pillar ensures that the group cannot choose to leave. The individual chose to buy the bond, once. After that, the bond ensures that the government cannot choose to default.

The deeper implication of this mechanism is that the commitment device always benefits whoever holds it — which means that whoever introduced the first commitment devices into human social organization accumulated power as a direct consequence. The people who first built stone pillars, in other words, were not just building a ritual space. They were building a machine for the production of obligation.

This is why the sunk cost trap is so difficult to escape at the civilizational level. The investment compounds over generations: each generation inherits not just the physical structures of the previous one but the social obligations those structures embody. The temple requires maintenance. The field requires replanting. The wall requires reinforcement. Each of these activities is a form of renewed commitment — a fresh investment that makes the previous investments more valuable, and that therefore makes the cost of abandonment higher. The trap tightens with each iteration, until the point at which leaving would mean losing everything — and at that point, no one leaves.

"The first stone pillar was not a religious object. It was an anchor. It tied a people to a place so completely that the place became who they were — and leaving became equivalent to dying."

— Dr. Mariana V. Ochsner, Archaeology and Political Theory, University of Vienna
